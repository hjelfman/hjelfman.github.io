# thank you

Most of last night was spent tossing and turning; my landlord turned off the heat again for some unknowable reason. Under my covers I get far too warm, but I become freezing without them as my hands turn purple. As the night cares not for comfort, it marched on until morning as my phone's alarm went off at 6am, before the sun rose. Before, one would have to use a special candle, rely on a window knocker, or their own intuition to wake up at any exact time. Rolling off my couch into the restroom, I used the toilet, which would have been a bedpan before city sewer systems became the norm. Getting dressed, I put on the same gym clothes I've worn for the past ten years made of bamboo fibers. These seem to last forever, keep me warm in the cold and dry in the summer. Before leaving, I slipped on my running shoes, which, while having been in much better condition, have survived about 500 miles of trail without much damage, seeming as if they will last for much longer. The modern running shoe is about one hundred years old; the foam which allows mine to last for so long is less than 25. We take these comforts for granted.

It was still dark as I left the house, the wrought-iron electric lamps of Central Park guiding me to the river. In less-lit areas, treading carefully so as not to miss a step. From there down the Hudson as buses and cars passed, quieter than I remembered—as a kid, most cars used internal combustion engines making quite a bit of noise when driven en masse; now, hybrids and electric are more common and much appreciated while listening to birdsong. The rest of the run continued in sub-50-degree weather as the sun rose, keeping me from breaking anything more than a light sweat. After six miles, I turned back home, thankful to be running on modern foam shoes and not rubber.

Getting back through the door, I poured some cashew milk and cocoa powder into a pot to prepare hot chocolate over a gas flame before bringing the shower to an instantaneous 120F. Any vestiges of the cold in my extremities left as the rising steam warmed the apartment. As I dried myself off, I applied deodorant, moisturizer, and retinol, all of which protect my skin from time and damage as those before had tried for generations. Afterward, I fished a few syringes down from a medicine cabinet so that I could take my medicine.

The first medicine is experimental, meant to heal my ankles, which had been damaged in a mosh pit two years ago. At one point I was unable to run or jump; a few weeks ago they still gave me pain. This medicine has healed what would have been a lifelong injury in other times. The other medicine is for my GI tract, which, when left untreated, causes malnutrition—some of my family had suffered for decades with this.

Pouring myself my now-warm hot chocolate (with whipped cream and cinnamon on top, of course), I turned on my computer and started working on digital blueprints, which have replaced the more easily lost physical copies in recent years, messaging a coworker across the country instantaneously as I did so.

Modernity is a blessing.
